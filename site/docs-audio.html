Hear the battle the way a crew does

TOS-1A launchers fire a rippling salvo in the Ironworks yard at night

Gunfire, impacts and engines sound from where they happen and arrive later the farther away they are. Hits are confirmed the way a crew experiences them: by the strike at the target and the gunner’s call.

8 min

Where the sound comes from

Gunfire, impacts, engines, tracks, ambience and the crew radio are first-party sounds generated for Claude of Tanks with ElevenLabs. Each generated sound was produced in takes that were measured and scored, and the best were mastered for their kind of sound; every voice take was also transcribed and checked against its script. No third-party recordings are sampled, and nothing is generated while you play.

Every sound you hear is one of these recordings: nothing is synthesized, not the alarms, not the radio's key clicks, not the weight under a blast. Audio starts with your first click, tap or key press, as browsers require. Each battle loads its core sounds as it opens and rarer ones on first use; a sound that is not ready yet, or that cannot play where WebM/Opus audio is unsupported (Safari before 17.4), stays silent rather than being replaced by a stand-in.

Gunfire and distance

Every weapon belongs to one of eighteen report classes: rifle and heavy machine guns; 20, 25, 30, 40 and 50 mm autocannons; 90, 105, 120, 125, 130 and 152 mm guns; anti-tank missile and heavy rocket launchers; and the AC-130's autocannon, howitzer and missiles. Each class has a close and a distant report that blend with range, followed by an echo that matches the battlefield: open ground, forest, town or mountains. A gun is heard as a real report: an instant crack, a short body that decays rather than swells, then its echo. Under it sits the punch of the muzzle blast, a recorded slam of air and the air rushing back behind it, tight from a machine gun and deep from a tank gun; machine-gun bursts echo once per beat.

Sound takes time to travel: a distant shot is seen before it is heard, and a far impact arrives late. The air softens distant sounds and hills between you and the source muffle them; on Mars sound is slower and thinner, and on the Moon, with no air, you hear little beyond your own hull. Gunfire fades far more gently than it would in life, so distant fights stay audible, and a shell passing close gives a flyby or a sharp near-miss crack.

Hits, kills and misses

No interface sound tells you a shot hit. You hear it at the target, from its direction, delayed by the distance. Up close an armor hit is tearing metal, a heavy clang or a dull thud; farther out, a distant crack, knock or clang with an outdoor echo. HE and HEAT add their blasts, reactive armor its cassettes. Your own rounds carry farther than other hits: a penetration at 700 m is a distant crack about two seconds after the shell lands.

Your kill is the target going up: blast, debris, turret and any cook-off, heard as far away as your hits. About half a second after a main-gun round lands, the time it takes to see it, your gunner almost always calls the result: in English, “Penetration”, “Ricochet”, “No penetration”, a module or crew hit, or “Target destroyed”. A miss is called “Short. Adjusting.” if the round fell short of the enemy it was laid on, otherwise “Miss.”

Gun, loader and sight

Your own gun has its own report, fuller than anyone else's, with the breech recoiling and running back into battery. The loader is heard in step with the reload: a manual loader opens the ready-rack door, pulls a round, shuts the door, rams it home and closes the breech; carousel and bustle autoloaders, autocannon feeds, missile tubes and launcher arms, and drum magazines refilled round by round each have their own sequence, ending in the lock that says the gun is ready. Your own tank adds the turret drive starting, running and braking, the elevation servo, cabin hum and rattle.

In the sight, your gun is heard as an interior report and your engine through the turret and headset, while the world outside dulls; your own gun follows the gunfire volume. A heavy round through your hull, or an HE burst or vehicle explosion close by, muffles everything; hearing returns over about four and a half seconds, with ringing in the ears after the strongest blasts. Your own gun never causes it, and Settings can turn it off.

Engine and running gear

Each hull carries the sound of its powertrain. The gas turbines of the Abrams and T-80 spool and whine rather than shift. The two-stroke diesels of the Leopard 1, Chieftain and T-64 lineage, Soviet and modern V12 diesels, the air-cooled diesels of the M48, M60 and Merkava 1 to 3, infantry fighting vehicle diesels and petrol V12s each sound different, with a turbo whistle or hybrid-electric drive where a vehicle has one.

Speed, throttle, slope and engine damage set the revs, and gearboxes hold each gear instead of hunting between two. Tracks change with the ground and your speed: earth, hard surfaces, mud, sand and snow, and wading through water. Brake squeal, skids, suspension landings, a damaged engine’s knock, stalls and restarts all have their own sounds. Nearby tanks are heard in this detail, distant ones more simply.

Crew radio

Each nation’s crews speak their own language over the intercom: American and British English, German, Russian, Ukrainian, Chinese, French, Swedish, Japanese, Korean, Italian, Polish and Hebrew, each with a commander and a crew voice. The calls are written in the terse procedure each army’s tank crews use, not translated word for word: a German crew calls a vehicle fire “Brand!” because “Feuer” is the order to fire.

Every line passes through a live intercom chain: a narrow headset band, compression, drive, static and squelch. A damaged radio module narrows the band, adds distortion and drops syllables. One transmission plays at a time: survival warnings cut in, calls that would arrive stale are dropped, and chatter is rationed, so autocannon and machine-gun hits are called only now and then. In Settings → Sound, Crew voices defaults to National crews, following each tank’s nation. The flag dropdown can instead use any one of the 13 existing national crews for every tank.

Battlefields and the garage

Each battlefield has its own soundscape: a stereo bed, a water or machinery layer where the map has one, and distant sounds at random bearings, mostly crows, hawks, gulls, wind, rubble and machinery, with songbirds, bees or a distant siren where they belong. In battle a distant war rumbles underneath. Artillery, flak, anti-aircraft fire and flyovers have their own sounds, as do trees, fences, walls, cars, crates and rubble when they break.

Every map also sets the reverb and the echo after each shot. The garage is a working hangar: a room tone with tools, impact wrenches, a hammer on a track pin, the crane chain, an air compressor, a diesel being run up and the big door a few metres away, all on the ambience volume. Every control in the garage and the menus has its sound, and garage sound starts with your first click or key press.

Mode sounds

In Drone mode an enemy drone buzzes where it flies, rising in pitch as it closes and inaudible beyond about 500 m. Yours spins up as it lifts off, and while it flies you hear the battle from the drone: its motors lead, from a hovering hum to a full-throttle buzz as they work harder, with the wind rising with speed and the feed's hiss growing toward the 850 m range limit and in the last ten seconds of battery. Your tank is just another vehicle out there, though a hit on it still reaches you. The feed cuts out in a burst of static when the drone strikes, is recalled or is lost, and you are back in the tank.

From the ground the AC-130 is heard as four turboprops circling overhead, its autocannon thumping and its howitzer booming from the sky. Its crew hears the cabin: the turboprops, the airframe rattling and the wind at the gun ports, the guns firing inside the fuselage, the howitzer's hot case clanging onto the deck before the crew rams the next round by hand, the missile leaving the wing pylon and the selector arming each gun. Each mode opens on its own sound: an air-raid siren for the Horde (and at every wave, with the all-clear after it), a distant barrage for the Frontline (again as the line moves up), a vault door for the Juggernaut (whose own engine and gun sound deeper and heavier than anyone's), broken radio for Infected, a foghorn for Turbo Ball, an armory for Gun Game and the gunship passing overhead for the AC-130; Realistic opens on silence. Caches land with a thud where they arrive. In Gun Game every stage advance plays the weapon changeover and the loader calls the new round; in Infected, being turned plays a grave sting and radio interference.

Sound settings

The Sound tab has six sliders: master, engine and mechanical, gunfire and impacts (including your own gun), ambience, interface and music, and crew voices and alarms. Changes apply at once and persist; releasing a slider plays a reference sound at the new level. Crew voices selects National crews or one nation’s voices for all tanks; the choice is saved across battles and reloads, and the concussion muffle and the critical-damage heartbeat can each be turned off.

The mix follows what you are doing. Gunfire and your own tank lead: its engine, its gun and the machinery inside it sit above everyone else's, and other engines, ambience, the radio and the interface sit beneath. Pausing lowers the battle, the killcam drops the live fight under its replay, and spectating and the garage have their own balance. To keep a full battle readable, at most 32 sounds play at once on desktop and 16 on mobile, and the lowest-priority sound gives way first.
